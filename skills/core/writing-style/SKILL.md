# Writing Style

> This skill is a template. The Mechanics, Structure, and Resonance sections are craft rules that transfer to anyone. The Voice section is one author's canon — replace it with yours. A voice skill you didn't rewrite is someone else's voice.

## The Sign Test — Write the Sign and Walk Away

Hard pre-publish gate against writing that's about being seen instead of being useful. Run before Voice/Mechanics — a piece can pass every style rule below and still fail this.

A signpost at a fork points the traveler down the road. It never signs the painter's name. The painter knew the sign was useful, planted it, and left — didn't wait around for credit, didn't need to know if anyone used it. The writing is the sign, not a monument to the sign-maker.

**Two failures:**

1. **Making it about you.** Insight timestamps ("I already knew," "I called this") — cut every one; a right idea needs no date stamp. Elegance built to be admired — if a line feels clever and you enjoy it, that's the warning light. The mirror "I" — every first-person line must be a window the reader climbs through ("I got stuck exactly here"), never a mirror the author admires himself in ("I figured out what everyone missed"). Test each "I": window or mirror? Mirrors get cut.

2. **Not walking away.** Write the true sentence, then stop — the extra paragraph that proves you're smart is the one to delete. No victory lap. End on the reader or the idea, never on your own cleverness.

**Public-platform tells** (a public stage = strongest pull toward both failures): the humblebrag open (scar decorating a trophy — cut the trophy); thought-leader posture (written to be seen wise, not to make one reader act); achievement disguised as lesson ("what I learned scaling X to Y" where Y is the point — strip the number; if the lesson dies without it, it was never the lesson).

**The gate:** Would this land the same with no byline and no likes? If it only works with your name attached, it's a monument, not a sign. Start again.

## Voice

Practitioner, not guru. First-person storytelling ("I"). Add "you" only where it pulls readers into application (forcing questions, binary choices, "when you see this pattern...").

Humble and factual. Commit to positions—don't hedge. Direct without being rude. Kind without being soft.

## Resonance (Made to Stick)

### Core Principle: Reader as Hero

You are not the hero. The reader is. Your job is to take them from where they are to where they could be. Every piece of writing answers: what does the reader gain or lose?

### The "This Is Me" Test

Resonance happens when readers recognize themselves. They think: "I felt exactly this" or "I got stuck exactly here."

To create recognition:

- **Name the specific moment.** Not "leaders struggle with feedback" but "you rehearsed the conversation six times in your head, then said nothing."
- **Use their language.** The words they say to themselves, not the words experts use about them.
- **Start where they are.** Situation in SCQA must be something they nod at—"yes, that's my world."

### Make It Stick: SUCCESs

Ideas that stick share six traits:

- **Simple:** One core idea, stripped to its essence. If you had to cut everything but one sentence, which survives?
- **Unexpected:** Violate expectations. Create a curiosity gap—open a question the reader needs answered.
- **Concrete:** Sensory, tangible details. Not "improve communication" but "the Slack message sat unread for three days."
- **Credible:** Testable. Human-scale statistics. "1 in 5 of your direct reports" not "20% of employees."
- **Emotional:** Make them feel. Appeal to identity ("people like us do things like this"), not just self-interest.
- **Stories:** Narrative is a flight simulator. Readers mentally rehearse the scenario you describe.

### Stakes

Every piece needs stakes. What happens if the reader ignores this? What do they gain if they act?

SCQA's "Complication" should carry weight. Not just "things changed" but "and here's what you'll lose if you don't adapt."

### Curiosity Gaps

Open a gap between what readers know and what they want to know. Then fill it.

- Tease the counterintuitive: "The best managers don't give feedback in the moment."
- Name the pattern they haven't seen: "There's a reason your top performer just disengaged."
- Promise the specific: "Three questions that surface the real objection."

Don't close the gap too fast. Let it pull them forward.

## The Iceberg Theory (Hemingway)

### Core Principle: Omit What You Know

If you know your subject deeply, you can leave things out. The reader will feel what's unsaid as strongly as if you'd stated it. What's beneath the surface gives weight to what's visible.

"The dignity of movement of an iceberg is due to only one-eighth of it being above water."

### Rules

- **Omit confidently.** If you leave something out because you know it, the story strengthens. If you leave it out because you don't know it, the story fails.
- **Trust the reader.** They will fill gaps. Don't hand-hold.
- **Show, don't explain.** Demonstrate through action and example, not declaration.
- **The quality of writing can be judged by what the author eliminated.**

### Application to Business Writing

This is the antidote to overexplaining. If you've done the thinking, you've earned the right to be brief. The reader senses the depth without needing every detail spelled out.

Test: If removing a sentence doesn't lose meaning, remove it.

### Starting Point

"Write one true sentence. Write the truest sentence you know."

If writing becomes elaborate or showy, stop. Start again with a true, declarative sentence.

## Structure (Minto Pyramid Principle)

### Core Rule: Lead with the Answer

State your conclusion first. Reader understands your main point within 30 seconds. Supporting ideas branch beneath.

Every document supports one single thought—the one that summarizes your final set of groupings.

### Introduction: SCQA

Hook the reader with a story structure:

- **Situation:** Context the reader knows and agrees with. Stable state. (Reader nods: "yes, that's my world.")
- **Complication:** What changed. The problem or opportunity. Creates urgency. (Stakes: what they lose if they ignore this.)
- **Question:** The question naturally raised by the complication. (Curiosity gap opens.)
- **Answer:** Your main point. The top of the pyramid. (Gap closes—but only partially. More gaps open in the body.)

### Grouping Ideas

**Three rules for any grouping:**

1. Ideas above must summarize ideas below
2. Ideas in each group must be the same kind (test: can you label with a plural noun—reasons, steps, problems?)
3. Ideas must be in logical order

**Four valid orders:**

- **Time:** Sequence, cause-effect, process steps
- **Structure:** Parts of a whole (org chart, geography, system components)
- **Importance:** Most important to least
- **Deductive:** Premise → premise → conclusion (use sparingly)

**MECE:** Mutually exclusive (no overlaps), collectively exhaustive (nothing missing).

**Limits:**

- Max 4 points in a deductive chain
- Max 5 points in an inductive group
- If you exceed these, you missed a grouping opportunity—rethink

### Summaries: No Blank Assertions

**Bad:** "There are three problems" or "We recommend five changes"

**Good:** State the effect of the actions or the significance of the similarity.

A summary tells the reader what the grouped ideas mean together—not just that they exist.

### Reasoning

- Use inductive at higher levels (easier to absorb; if one point fails, others stand)
- Use deductive only at paragraph level
- Present action before argument (unless reader will strongly disagree)

### Headings

- Headings remind, not dominate. Keep them concise.
- Never only one of any element (one subsection, one bullet)
- Parallel form—same grammatical structure at same level
- Don't depend on headings to carry your message
- Prose should flow without headings; opening sentence signals new topic

## Mechanics

### Words

- 8th-grade readable
- Simple words over complex (Anglo-Saxon over Latinate)
- Specific over general
- Concrete over abstract
- Data over adjectives

### Sentences

**Breathing:** Readers mentally breathe through sentences—even silently. Short sentence: quick breath, lands hard. Long sentence: deep breath held until the period, then exhale. Monotony happens when every sentence takes the same breath. Vary the rhythm. Let readers catch their breath after a long sentence with a short one. Build tension with quick punches, then release with a longer flow.

**Preserve natural rhythm.** When the raw material has natural cadence from the author's voice — staccato repetition, punchy declarations, deliberate parallel structure — preserve it. Mechanical checks (em dash limits, sentence length variation) are subordinate to force and cadence. Don't flatten a piece that hits hard just to pass a checklist. The author's rhythm is a feature, not a bug.

- Active voice
- Positive statements over negative ("this is poor" not "this is not good")
- Short sentences by default; longer ones must earn their place
- No more than three sentences of similar length in a row
- No weasel words: should, might, could, probably, significant, generally, usually
- Polysyndeton sparingly: "and...and...and" to accelerate action

### Paragraphs

- Topic sentence first
- Supporting sentences that explain, build, or prove
- Closing sentence that emphasizes or shows consequence
- One idea per paragraph

### Formatting

- Prose over bullets
- Bullets only when: (a) user requests, or (b) content is genuinely list-shaped
- If using bullets, each item earns its place—no padding
- Tables and numbered steps for frameworks
- Get to the core fast

## What to Cut

- Adjectives (unless essential)
- Adverbs (almost always cut)
- Filler phrases ("it's important to note that", "in order to")
- Examples that don't add new information
- Defensive paragraphs (explaining what's already clear)
- Overexplaining—if you know it well enough to omit it, omit it
- Background sections not tied to a question
- Mystery-story structure (building to a reveal)
- Anything the reader can infer

## Anti-Repetition (DRY)

Say each thing once, at full strength, in its strongest place. Repetition is the most common way good writing goes slack, and the surest sign the writer didn't re-read. Four kinds, four checks. Run all four in Polish; flag them in Coach.

### 1. Same idea, restated

One point, one home. Make a claim, then make it again later in different words, and the second instance steals force from the first. Keep the strongest phrasing; cut the rest.

Check: for each core claim, point to the single sentence that carries it. If two sentences carry the same claim, one goes.

### 2. Same word or phrase

Pet words recur invisibly to the writer — connectors ("so", "and so", "the thing is"), verbs ("drive", "unlock", "build"), a favorite metaphor. Three uses of the same distinctive word in one piece is a pattern.

Check: scan for any distinctive word used 3+ times; vary or cut. The exception is deliberate anaphora, where repetition is the device — see Mechanics → Preserve natural rhythm.

### 3. Same structure

Governed by the `human-proofing` skill's Structural Tells (sandwich format, constant parallelism, closing formula, subtitle pattern). Fix it there. Not restated here.

### 4. Across pieces

The repeat you cannot see from inside one draft. Newsletter to newsletter, post to post, you recycle openings, frames, and signature claims. The fix is a read, not a resolution:

- Before drafting a recurring piece (newsletter, blog, social post), read your last 3–4 published pieces. List the openings, central frames, and headline claims already used.
- Don't reuse them. Earn a fresh angle, or build on a prior piece by naming it explicitly.

Check: name what this piece says that the last three didn't. If you can't, it's a re-run — change the angle before drafting further.

## What to Avoid

- Marketing language, hype, promotional words
- AI-giveaway phrases: "dive into", "unleash", "game-changing", "leverage", "elevate"
- Forced friendliness
- Non-committal hedging
- Unnecessary politeness
- Clichés, jargon, hashtags, emojis
- Conditional language (could, might, may) when certainty is possible
- Intellectually blank summaries
- Illogical ordering that forces readers to jump back and forth
- Hand-holding the reader through obvious conclusions

## Human-Proofing (Anti-AI Patterns)

AI-polished writing has recognizable tells. Structure and clarity come from AI. Humanity comes from the author. These rules ensure the polish doesn't sterilize the person. (The full detection layer lives in the `human-proofing` skill — this is the summary that applies during drafting.)

### Structural Tells to Break

**No sandwich format.** Don't follow the same skeleton every time (hook → framework → numbered items → closing question). Vary the shape. Some pieces should open with a story and never reach a framework. Some should start with the conclusion. Some should end abruptly without a neat bow. If every piece reads like a template was filled in, readers notice.

**No constant parallelism.** "It's not X, it's Y" is powerful once. Twice in a piece is a pattern. Three times is a machine. Limit to one per piece, maximum. Find other ways to create contrast — a story that shows the difference, a question that makes the reader feel it, a before/after example.

**Vary closings.** Not every piece ends with "What's your experience?" or an engagement question. Some pieces earn a quiet ending — a final image, a single sentence that sits heavy, or just stopping when the point is made. Engagement questions are fine sometimes. But when every piece ends with one, it becomes formula.

**Break the subtitle pattern.** Not every subtitle should be "Why [claim]" or "An Operator's Guide to [topic]." Mix it up. Use a phrase from the piece. Use a question. Use nothing.

### Sentence-Level Tells to Catch

**Em dashes must earn their place.** Every em dash should do something a period or comma cannot. If the em dash is decorative — just creating a pause that a period would handle — replace it. Limit to 2-3 per piece. More than that flags AI.

**No symmetrical sentence pairs.** "AI accelerates tasks. Humans bring intuition." — this balanced, mirrored structure is an AI fingerprint. Real writers don't naturally produce matched pairs. If you see two sentences that mirror each other in structure and length, rewrite one of them.

**No rhetorical questions as transitions.** "So why does this matter?" and "But what happens when...?" are filler. Cut them. Transition by starting the next thought directly. The reader doesn't need a question to follow you to the next paragraph.

**Blogging clichés to kill on sight:** "Have you ever wondered," "Here's the thing," "Let me explain," "Without further ado," "It goes without saying," "Let's dive in," "Here's the kicker," "Ready to [verb] your [noun]?"

### The Mess Test

Before any piece is final, check: where is the real-world mess?

- **Is there a moment where something didn't work?** Not a hypothetical failure — a real one, with a name or a date or a specific detail.
- **Is there a "yeah, but in reality..." moment?** Where the clean framework meets the messy world and the writer admits the gap.
- **Is there tonal variation?** Does the piece have a moment of frustration, doubt, humor, or surprise — not just steady instructional confidence?
- **Is there at least one tangent, aside, or unexpected detail?** Something a reader wouldn't predict from the heading. Something only a person who lived this would include.

If the answer is no to all four, the piece sounds like AI regardless of the words used. Coach the author to add these before polishing further.

### Voice Switching

AI tends to stay in one voice (all second person, or all third person) from start to finish. Human writers switch naturally.

- Mix first-person stories ("I was sitting in the review...") with second-person application ("When you see this pattern...")
- Occasionally shift to third person for distance ("The manager who does this...")
- The switch itself signals a human mind at work

## Before You Send — the Validation Gate

Runs on every deliverable, and *while* you're still building it — the over-producing pattern shows up as you add, not only at the final click. Before sending anything to leadership or stakeholders, and any time you catch yourself adding "one more thing":

- **Values or validation?** Finish the sentence: "I'm sending this because ___." If it's *to help them solve X*, send. If it's *to show I thought of everything*, cut it in half. If it's *so [person] sees I'm valuable*, wait ten minutes, then decide.
- **Contribution over coverage.** What can you — and only you — add that makes a real difference? Ship that. Cut the rest.
- **Problem before solution.** Have they agreed on the problem? If not, send the one-page problem, not the six-page answer, and let them build the solution with you.

## Collaboration Modes

**Mode 1: "Write it for me"**
The user gives the idea. Claude drafts in this style. Used when speed matters.

**Mode 2: "Coach" (default)**
The user writes. Claude asks questions. Build muscle, no drafting.

When coaching, also check for AI tells:
- Flag repetition (Anti-Repetition / DRY): same idea restated, a distinctive word used 3+ times, or a frame recycled from a recent piece
- Flag parallelism overuse ("you've used 'It's not X, it's Y' twice — find another way")
- Ask "where's the mess?" if every section is clean and resolved
- Ask "what went wrong when you tried this?" if no failure story exists
- Challenge generic sections: "Can you name a specific person, date, or place here?"
- Flag decorative em dashes and symmetrical sentence pairs

**Mode 3: "Polish"**
The user provides a draft. Claude goes paragraph by paragraph:

- Comment on problems
- Suggest cuts
- Flag overexplaining (Iceberg test: can this be omitted?)
- Tighten sentences
- Check grouping logic (MECE, same kind, logical order)
- Check resonance (Does reader see themselves? Are stakes clear? Any curiosity gaps?)
- **Run Anti-Repetition (DRY) — all four checks:** same idea restated · distinctive word 3+ times · same structure (defer to human-proofing) · across-pieces (read the last 3–4 published pieces before drafting)
- Stress-test reasoning (chain `antifragile-advisor`)
- **Run Human-Proofing checks:** parallelism count, em dash count, sandwich format, mess test, voice switching, symmetrical sentences, rhetorical question transitions, closing formula
- **Run the Sign Test — mandatory, run last:** insight timestamps, mirror-"I"s, victory laps, self-recentering closers. Apply the gate: would this land with no byline and no likes? Fail → flag exact lines, don't just pass/fail the whole piece.
- **If the piece passes all structure/resonance checks but fails human-proofing, flag it:** "This reads clean but sounds like AI. Here's what needs your fingerprint."

**Mode 4: "Refraction" (one text → 5 audiences)**
The user has a piece written for one reader. Rewrite it for 5 audiences at once to pressure-test the idea — seeing it refracted teaches more about the idea than any single rewrite. Fully commit to each; different vocabulary, tone, emphasis, structure.

- **Beginner** — no jargon, analogies, assume intelligent-but-new
- **Domain expert** — skip basics, precise terms, go deeper than the original (they notice oversimplification)
- **Skeptic** — lead with strongest evidence, pre-empt the objection before they raise it
- **Journalist** — one hook headline + 2 sentences, most newsworthy angle
- **Executive** — 3 sentences max: what it is, why it matters, what to do

End with: which version was hardest to write well and why (reveals where the idea is weakest). Use as a thinking tool, not just a deliverable.

Trigger phrases:

- "Write this for me" → Mode 1
- "Coach me" → Mode 2
- "Polish this" → Mode 3
- "Refract this" / "5 audiences" / "rewrite for different readers" → Mode 4
